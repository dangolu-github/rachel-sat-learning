3. Phrases at the Edges of a Sentence
The idea · Phrases that hang on an IC

1. Phrase

  • A phrase works as one part of speech: a noun, an adjective or an adverb.
  • It has no verb-and-subject pair, so it is not a clause.
  • It cannot stand alone: it hangs on an IC.

2. Kinds of phrase

  • Prepositional phrase: a preposition and its object (after the storm).
  • Participial phrase (-ing / -ed): works as an adjective (reducing travel time; built in 1902).
  • Infinitive phrase (to + verb): a noun, adjective or adverb (to test the model).
  • Appositive phrase: a noun phrase that renames a noun (a task that took six years).

3. Why it needs an IC

  • The -ing or -ed form in a verbal phrase is not a finite verb (the Grammar rule on -ing / -ed forms: on their own they are not the verb of a clause).
  • So a phrase at the start or end of a sentence attaches to the IC, like a DC.

  • The bridge reopenedIC, reducing travel time for local residentsphrase.
  • The team mapped the whole coastlineIC, a task that took six yearsphrase.

  1. Does the stretch have a verb with its own subject?
  2. No → a phrase: one comma where it meets the IC.
  3. Never a period or a semicolon in front of a phrase.

SAT Questions

Question 6 · C8
In the late 1960s, researchers discovered symmetrical magnetic stripes on either side of mid-ocean ridges, providing compelling proof that tectonic plates drift apart as new crust ______ finding that helped unify disparate observations into the modern theory of plate tectonics.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  1. forms, a
  2. forms; a
  3. forms. A
  4. forms a
Worked Answer

A. forms, a

  1. The IC is complete at “as new crust forms”.
  2. “a finding that helped unify disparate observations …” has no finite verb of its own (“helped” belongs to “that”): it is an appositive, a noun phrase that renames the whole discovery.
  3. A closing phrase that comments on the whole clause takes a comma before it → A.

4. Core and Extra
The idea · Core and extra

1. Core

  • Every IC has a core: the subject, its finite verb, and what completes the verb (an object or complement).
  • A single comma never cuts the core (Unit 6).

2. Extra

  • Extra (nonessential): a part you can lift out — the sentence stays grammatical and means the same.
  • Extra information is set off like a parenthesis: the same mark at both ends.
  • At the end of a sentence, the period is the closing mark.

3. What can be extra

  • a phrase: according to the report
  • an appositive phrase: a biologist at the institute
  • a DC — an adjective clause: it describes a noun and opens with a relative pronoun (who, whom, whose, which, that): which contain maps

  2. Still a sentence, same meaning → extra: close it with the mark that opened it.
  3. The sentence breaks or the meaning changes → core: no commas.
